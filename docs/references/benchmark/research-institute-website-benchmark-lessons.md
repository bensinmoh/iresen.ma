# Research & Technology Institute Websites — Lessons Learned

> **Purpose:** Reusable lessons on content, information architecture, user experience, and page layout distilled from a comparative review of research and innovation institutions.
>
> **Scope:** This is a **benchmark-derived reference**, not a sitemap, page specification, proposed copy, design concept, or instruction to imitate any institution. Use it to evaluate editorial and layout decisions alongside the project's own approved requirements and identity.
>
> **Institutions reviewed:** INESC TEC, TECNALIA, CEA / CEA-Liten, Fraunhofer, EnergyVille, NREL / National Laboratory of the Rockies, InnoEnergy, and TÜBİTAK MAM.

## 1. Core patterns

1. **Explain, demonstrate, connect.** Effective institutional websites explain their role, substantiate their capabilities through real work, and make the next interaction clear.
2. **Lead with purpose and value.** Visitors understand the challenge addressed and the value created before encountering organisational history or internal structure.
3. **Make research tangible.** Themes are linked to programmes, projects, infrastructures, expertise, technologies, publications, partners, and outcomes.
4. **Provide multiple entry points into one body of knowledge.** A visitor may begin with a research theme, an application, a facility, a technology, or a collaboration need; the underlying records remain connected.
5. **Differentiate institutional authority from promotional language.** Evidence, precise descriptions, and credible results communicate competence better than broad claims or superlatives.
6. **Design for different levels of expertise.** Offer a clear overview first, then progressive access to technical depth.

## 2. Information architecture and navigation

- **Topic-oriented navigation is easier to understand than department-oriented navigation.** Public-facing categories describe subjects, capabilities, and interactions, not reporting lines.
- **A small number of distinct top-level concepts is easier to scan.** Avoid parallel categories whose boundaries a first-time visitor cannot infer.
- **A consistent content taxonomy reduces confusion.** A research theme, research focus, programme, project, platform, technology asset, and publication represent different entities and should not be used interchangeably.
- **Cross-links are essential, not decorative.** They reveal relationships between content types and allow exploration without duplicating entire pages.
- **Search and filters become valuable when a collection grows.** Project, publication, technology, and platform libraries benefit from relevant, consistently populated metadata.
- **Deep navigation and large mega-menus can expose institutional complexity instead of reducing it.** Navigation depth should reflect actual visitor needs, not organisational breadth.
- **The same content should not be maintained separately across sections.** Structured records and relationships allow a project, for example, to appear under a programme, a theme, a platform, and a related result without conflicting versions.

### Distinctions that remain useful

| Content entity | Primary question it answers |
| --- | --- |
| Research theme | What challenge or knowledge domain is being addressed? |
| Research focus / axis | Which specific questions and intended advances sit within the theme? |
| Programme | How is work coordinated toward defined goals? |
| Project | What is being done, with whom, and with which results? |
| Platform / laboratory | What can be developed, tested, measured, or demonstrated here? |
| Technology / intellectual-property asset | What solution or know-how exists, how mature is it, and how may it be used? |
| Publication / resource | What findings, methods, data, or insights can be consulted? |
| Partnership mechanism | How can an outside organisation participate or collaborate? |
| Impact story | What changed because of the work, and what evidence supports that change? |

These entities may relate to one another, but are not all levels of the same hierarchy.

## 3. Narrative and editorial content

- **Purpose precedes institutional detail.** Strong opening content communicates the external challenge, the institution's role, and its contribution.
- **Descriptions move from accessible to specialist.** Short explanations precede methodology, equipment inventories, technical specifications, or administrative details.
- **Headings convey information.** Descriptive headings are more useful than generic labels such as “Our excellence,” “Innovation,” or “Discover more.”
- **Outcomes and applications clarify abstract research.** Explain what knowledge, device, process, service, or capability emerges and where it may matter.
- **Named examples validate broad institutional claims.** Specific projects, technologies, collaborations, facilities, and documented results act as evidence.
- **Editorial tone should be confident but measured.** Avoid inflated impact claims, undefined superlatives, and promises that are not supported by the content.
- **Numbers need context.** Every statistic should have a unit, scope, reference period, definition, and preferably an identifiable source.
- **Inputs, activities, outputs, and outcomes are not synonyms.** Funding and equipment are inputs; calls and projects are activities; prototypes and publications are outputs; adoption and measurable benefits are outcomes.

## 4. Research themes, programmes, and projects

### Research-theme pages

Observed useful content sequence:

1. The energy, industrial, environmental, or scientific challenge.
2. The scope of the research theme in plain language.
3. Main research questions or focus areas.
4. Examples of programmes and projects.
5. Related capabilities, facilities, teams, and publications.
6. Evidence of results or practical applications.
7. A clear route to deeper information or contact.

### Programme pages

- State the programme's objective and the problem it is intended to address.
- Distinguish programme-wide priorities from individual project activities.
- Associate relevant projects and partners.
- Show status, timing, and results when available.
- Avoid implying that a programme is itself a physical facility, single project, or funding mechanism unless that is genuinely its nature.

### Project pages

Effective project records consistently expose:

- A descriptive title and concise summary.
- The challenge or need.
- The approach or work undertaken.
- Partners and roles.
- Dates and project status.
- Financing or programme affiliation, if publishable and relevant.
- Technical or scientific results, with evidence where possible.
- Associated themes, facilities, outputs, publications, or technologies.
- A named contact or appropriate institutional contact route.

**Layout lesson:** An overview, a few meaningful facts, strong supporting imagery, and related records make projects easier to scan than uninterrupted descriptive prose.

## 5. Platforms and laboratories

- **Capabilities come before equipment.** External visitors first need to know what can be tested, validated, developed, or demonstrated.
- **Application scenarios make infrastructure understandable.** Test types, demonstrable use cases, supported technologies, and user groups explain why a facility matters.
- **Specifications are supporting evidence.** Technical instruments, operating ranges, standards, and capacity remain available without dominating introductory sections.
- **Access must be intelligible.** Visitors should be able to distinguish research collaboration, service provision, and other modes of engagement when those modes exist.
- **Facilities are connected to their research context.** Associated projects, themes, teams, and results demonstrate actual use.
- **Authentic photography and schematics serve different purposes.** Photography establishes the physical reality and scale of an infrastructure; simplified diagrams explain a process, operating principle, or system relationship.

## 6. Technology transfer and intellectual property

- **A patent count is not a technology portfolio.** The value of an asset catalogue lies in discoverability and clarity about applications and readiness.
- **Each technology is presented as a potential solution.** Useful fields include the problem addressed, technical principle, distinctive features, application domains, maturity, and supporting evidence.
- **IP terminology must be precise.** A filed application, published application, granted patent, active right, and lapsed right are different statuses; the interface should not conflate them.
- **Maturity and availability are separate questions.** Technical readiness does not automatically indicate licensing availability or freedom to operate.
- **Searchable portfolios benefit from structured metadata.** Domains, keywords, maturity, asset type, and availability can support browsing where data is reliable.
- **The next step is explicit.** Technology enquiries, licensing discussions, joint development, and startup/spin-off exploration are different interactions.
- **Confidentiality matters.** Public asset pages reveal enough to assess relevance without disclosing protected know-how or confidential partner information.

## 7. Partnerships and collaboration pathways

- **People arrive with a need, not an internal service category.** A successful collaboration experience maps recognisable visitor objectives to suitable mechanisms.
- **Distinct needs imply distinct pathways.** Collaborative research, access to facilities, technology licensing, institutional cooperation, and skills development have different expectations and processes.
- **Specific calls to action outperform generic contact prompts.** The intended action should match the page: enquire about a technology, discuss a project, access a facility, or explore a partnership.
- **Explain what happens next.** Eligibility, contact point, stages of collaboration, expected information, and any applicable constraints build confidence.
- **Show collaboration through evidence.** Actual partner projects, co-developed technologies, and documented results substantiate claims of openness.
- **Do not over-commercialise a public-interest mission.** Collaboration language can be actionable without portraying every research activity as a product for sale.

## 8. Results, impact, and credibility

- **Proof belongs near the claim it supports.** A concrete example close to a mission statement or capability description is stronger than an isolated metrics page.
- **Project results and impact stories are complementary.** A project record explains what was done; an impact story explains the external change that followed.
- **Case studies have a recognisable structure:** initial challenge → intervention → partners → delivered result → observed benefit.
- **Metrics should distinguish output from impact.** Project counts, patents, publications, prototypes, licenses, adoption, and industrial outcomes have different meanings.
- **Attribution and evidence matter.** Describe the institution's contribution accurately, particularly in multi-partner projects.
- **Visible dates support credibility.** Research status, achievements, programme timelines, and publications should be time-stamped and reviewed.
- **Strong visuals are evidence when authentic.** Real prototypes, demonstrators, equipment, people at work, and results can communicate achievements more effectively than generic stock imagery.

## 9. Publications and knowledge resources

- **A publication library is a retrieval interface, not a news feed.** Users benefit from filtering by topic, date, type, and author or programme where relevant.
- **A resource has stable metadata.** Title, summary, publication date, content type, authors/owners, and access link should be consistent.
- **Editorial and technical content serve different levels of understanding.** An accessible summary can lead to a full scientific report, dataset, article, or publication.
- **Avoid mixed-content ambiguity.** News, events, research outputs, reports, and open calls are distinct types even when they appear in a unified search.
- **Related-content links create continuity.** A finding can connect back to the project, programme, theme, or facility that generated it.

## 10. Visual layout and page composition

### Shared layout lessons

- **Clear hierarchy beats decorative density.** A readable headline, short introduction, and a limited set of primary actions establish orientation.
- **Progressive disclosure supports both scanning and deep reading.** Overview sections lead to cards, structured details, expandable technical material, and dedicated records as appropriate.
- **Alternating visual rhythm improves long pages.** Use a deliberate balance of short text blocks, relevant imagery, evidence, and structured collections; avoid monotonous walls of identical cards.
- **Card design should reflect content type.** A project card, technology asset, publication, and laboratory need different metadata even if they share consistent visual components.
- **Imagery must communicate something real.** Photos, technical diagrams, illustrations, and data visualisations each have distinct purposes; decorative media should not compete with core information.
- **Visual consistency enables comprehension.** Typography, spacing, component patterns, image ratios, metadata treatment, and action labels should behave predictably.
- **Whitespace and content density need balance.** Institutional credibility benefits from calm layouts, but excessive empty space can bury essential evidence and slow information retrieval.
- **Prominent figures require supporting definitions.** Large metrics are effective when labels, periods, and scope make them trustworthy.
- **Each section should earn its place.** Sections that repeat the same claim or function primarily as visual filler weaken the visitor journey.

### Common reusable page anatomy

The recurring pattern across effective pages is:

`Context / purpose → key information → capabilities or activities → evidence / examples → related content → relevant next action`

This is a **content-flow pattern**, not a mandatory sequence or a fixed visual template. Different page types emphasise different stages.

## 11. Interaction, accessibility, and multilingual content

- **Predictability is more valuable than novelty.** Familiar navigation, clear filters, descriptive links, and recognisable actions reduce cognitive load.
- **Search and filters require meaningful metadata.** A polished filtering interface cannot compensate for incomplete or inconsistent content records.
- **Accessibility is structural.** Heading hierarchy, text contrast, keyboard interaction, focus visibility, meaningful alt text, labelled controls, and readable responsive layouts are core quality attributes.
- **Motion should explain or orient.** Animation that competes with reading or reduces performance undermines serious technical content; reduced-motion preferences should be respected.
- **Responsive design preserves hierarchy, not merely dimensions.** Navigation, data tables, project facts, scientific figures, filters, and calls to action all need deliberate small-screen treatment.
- **Multilingual layouts need genuine adaptation.** Translation changes string lengths and text direction; right-to-left languages require direction-aware alignment, navigation, icon positioning where meaningful, and typography testing.
- **Technical terminology needs governance.** Research themes, programme titles, abbreviations, and IP statuses should remain consistent across languages and content types.

## 12. Recurring anti-patterns

- Leading with an organisational chart before explaining public value.
- Reproducing internal departmental structures as the main navigation.
- Confusing themes, programmes, projects, facilities, and technology assets.
- Treating a collection of project headlines as a coherent research strategy.
- Describing laboratory equipment without explaining usable capabilities.
- Calling all patent filings “patented technologies” or implying grant/availability without verification.
- Presenting project volume, spending, and publications as if they were demonstrated socioeconomic impact.
- Offering only a generic contact form when visitors have clearly different collaboration intentions.
- Publishing standalone content items without links to related projects, programmes, or results.
- Using promotional adjectives in place of concrete evidence.
- Making every section a visually identical card grid, regardless of content purpose.
- Using generic imagery that has little connection to the institution's actual activity.
- Creating a complex library interface before the underlying metadata is dependable.
- Using deep menus and duplicated content to represent organisational complexity.
- Making publication pages hard to search or mixing them indiscriminately with institutional news.

## 13. Benchmark-based review questions

Use these as evaluation questions—not as new feature requirements:

- Can a first-time visitor understand the institution's purpose and contribution without knowing its structure?
- Can a researcher reach relevant programmes, projects, facilities, and publications from a research topic?
- Can an industry visitor understand what a laboratory can actually do?
- Can a prospective technology partner understand what an asset does, its documented status, and how to enquire?
- Are the differences between a theme, programme, project, platform, and technology visible in labels and templates?
- Does each important institutional claim have a supporting example or verified metric?
- Does each substantial content page have meaningful related items and an appropriate next action?
- Can complex technical material be explored without overwhelming the introductory view?
- Are filtering, navigation, visual patterns, and terminology consistent across all content collections?
- Are layouts and components usable across mobile, keyboard navigation, and all supported languages?
